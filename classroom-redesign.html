Redesigning the Classroom

A student-survey-driven redesign of my middle school computer science classroom, focused on lighting, collaborative seating, and a dedicated robotics arena.

Starting With the Users

When I took over the CS classroom at Robert Adams, I inherited a space that worked the way most middle school classrooms work: fluorescent lights, rows of identical tables, and a single open floor that students treated as either a hallway or an obstacle. Before I changed anything, I wanted to know what my students actually thought about the room they spent thirty hours a week in.

I ran a short survey across all my sections asking three questions. What about this room makes it hard to focus? What about this room makes it hard to work with other people? If you could change one thing, what would it be? The answers were remarkably consistent. The lights were too bright and too uniform. There was nowhere to actually sit with a group. And the robotics work, which involved kits and motors and field pieces, was happening on the same tables as everything else, which meant cleanup ate into class time and projects rarely got finished.

What I Changed

Lighting

Swapped the harsh overhead fluorescents for warmer, dimmable task lighting in the work zones, with brighter zones near the front for direct instruction. Students consistently said the new lighting made the room feel calmer, especially during longer build sessions.

Seating Areas

Broke up the row layout into distinct zones: a soft-seating corner for reading and small-group discussion, collaborative tables for group projects, and individual desks for focused work. Students can choose where to sit based on what they're doing, which has been a quiet but real shift in how the room feels.

Robotics Arena

Carved out a dedicated robotics zone with a permanent field, organized kit storage, and a workbench for assembly. Projects stay set up between classes instead of getting torn down every period, which means students can iterate over multiple sessions and actually finish what they start.

What I Learned

The most useful thing about running a survey first was that it reframed the project for me. The redesign wasn't about my taste or what looked good. It was about removing friction the students could already name. Most of the changes I would have prioritized on my own (more wall storage, a different desk layout) didn't show up in the survey at all. Most of what students wanted (warmer lighting, a place to sit and talk, a robotics zone that didn't get disassembled) wasn't on my list. Listening first made the work better.
